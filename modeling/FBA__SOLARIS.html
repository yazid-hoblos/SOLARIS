Constraint-based modeling of metabolically engineered S.elongatus¶

Introduction to flux balance analysis¶

FBA is a mathematical technique used to estimate the flow of metabolites through the biochemical reactions of a given metabolic network. It is widely used in the field of metabolic engineering, as it enables the prediction of phenotypes such as cellular growth rates or the production rates of specific metabolites. It is a very powerful and modeling strategies, FBA does not require knowledge of kinetic parameters, which are often difficult to experimentally determine. A didactic explanation of FBA is provided in the primer "What is flux balance analysis" by Palsson, et al[1].

Representing metabolic networks¶

A genome scale metabolic network is a graph representing all possible reactions happening in a given organism, along with the genes encoding the enzymes carrying out these reactions. In our study, we utilize a previously published metabolic model of Synechococcus elongatus, which was constructed using established methodologies for genome-scale metabolic reconstruction. This constraint-based model represents the complete set of biochemical reactions occurring in the cell and is mathematically formulated as a stoichiometric matrix, where each entry corresponds to the stoichiometric coefficient of a given metabolite in a specific reaction. Metabolic networks are usually reconstructed from structural and functional genome annotations.

Such models can be represented as graphs or as numbers (add a visualize representation of a network).

Defining constraints¶

The model is subject to two main types of constraints, equilibrium and flux bounds.

  • Steady-state assumption: For each metabolite, the total rate of production must equal the total rate of consumption.
  • Flux bounds: Each reaction can operate only within specified upper and lower bounds, representing physiological relevant or experimentally determined limits.

More constraints can be defines. For example, genetic perturbations, such as gene knockouts, can be simulated in the model by setting the flux through associated reactions to zero. This allows for in silico prediction of gene essentiality and the impact of genetic modifications on metabolic performance.

Defining an objective function¶

An essential component of FBA is the objective function, which defines the cellular phenotype to be optimized. This function assigns a weight to each reaction, indicating its contribution to the phenotype of interest. For example, if the goal is to maximize growth, a weight of 1 is assigned to the biomass production reaction, and all other reactions are assigned a weight of 0. If a dual objective is considered—such as maximizing both growth and the production of a specific compound—then both the biomass and product-forming reactions are given non-zero weights. In FBA, the biomass reaction often serves as the objective function that is maximized to simulate the optimal growth rate of the organism. By optimizing the flux through the biomass reaction, FBA predicts the maximal growth rate under a given set of environmental conditions. (Explain more the maths based on Palsson's paper).

The biomass reaction is one of the most critical reactions in genome-scale metabolic models as it represents the overall process of cellular growth. It does not actually corresponds to a biochemical reaction happening in the cell. Rather, it summarizes all the reactions that contribute to the production of biomass components necessary for growth, such as proteins, lipids, nucleotides, and other macromolecules, as well as the steochiometry of each of these components.

Experimental data usually help refine the biomass reaction by providing emprirical estimates of the actual composition of biomass in the organism. This may involve determining the stoichiometric coefficients of the biomass components, such as the number of amino acids making up proteins or the number of lipids making up the membrane.

Running flux balance analysis¶

We are now all set to run our flux balance analysis. Before making any addition or deletion in the model, we can perform a flux balance analysis (FBA) with the growth as an objective function, to see what the maximum biomass production of the strain in its current state and the way the metabolic fluxes are shared between all metabolic pathways. By default, the objective function is the maximization of the growth of the cell, represented by the reaction of biomass production. The objective function is represented as coefficients, with 1 for the reaction that will be maximized by FBA and 0 for the reactions that are not maximized. Consequently, as the default value the coefficient of the biomass reaction is 1 and the coefficient for all other reactions are set to 0.

In [9]:
solution_WT = model_PCC7942.optimize()
print(f"Status: {solution_WT.status}") # Indicates whether solutions were found
print(f"Biomass production: {solution_WT.objective_value:.5f}")

Adding the 3HPP cycle to the metabolic network of the strain¶

We are now going to add the enzymes of the 3HPP cycle to the original genome-scale metabolic network of S.elongatus PCC7942. First, we load the model (under a a different name than the WT model).

In [31]:
model_PCC7942_with_gene_addition = load_model("iJB785")

We create a function that checks whether a metabolite already exists in a model, and creates it if it doesn't exist.

In [32]:
def retrieve_or_create_metabolite(model_PCC7942_with_gene_addition, met_id, name, compartment="c"):
    try:
        return model_PCC7942_with_gene_addition.metabolites.get_by_id(met_id)
    except KeyError:
        m = Metabolite(id=met_id, name=name, compartment=compartment)
        model_PCC7942_with_gene_addition.add_metabolites([m])
        return m

Then in order to add enzymes to the model, we create a reaction and set upper and lower bounds for the fluxes of these reactions. Then we add metabolites to these reactions by setting their stoechiometric coefficients (negative for substrates and negative for products). Finally we add the reactions to the network.

  • the alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGA) that would otherwise consume glyoxylate

    # Copy the model fresh
    model_temp = model_PCC7942_with_gene_deletion.copy()
    
    rxn = model_temp.reactions.get_by_id(rxn_id)
    genes_to_delete = [gene.id for gene in rxn.genes]
    
    # Knock out all genes for this enzyme
    for gene_id in genes_to_delete:
        try:
            gene = model_temp.genes.get_by_id(gene_id)
            gene.knock_out()
        except KeyError:
            print(f"Gene {gene_id} not found in the model!")

    # Run FBA
    solution = model_temp.optimize()
    
    results.append({
        'Enzyme_Reaction': rxn_id,
        'Deleted_Genes': genes_to_delete,
        'Growth': solution.objective_value
    })

import pandas as pd
df_results = pd.DataFrame(results)

print(df_results)
